\begin{abstract}
The one-step oracle for risk-limiting financial audits samples transactions
in proportion to their monetary misstatement contributions. We prove that
repeating this rule can require nearly the entire population to be reviewed
when competing fully supported policies have expected review counts
arbitrarily close to one. For every population size $N\ge2$, risk limit, and
finite uniform stake cap, the sharp worst-case ratio is $N$ for the original
non-control-variate betting confidence set with logical and running
intersections. The construction applies to every valid predictable betting
rule obeying the cap. A large transaction with a small misstatement
contribution is essential for reducing uncertainty, yet the oracle postpones
it. With review-cost ratio at most $\kappa$, the sharp expected-cost ratio is
$1+(N-1)\kappa$. The supremum ranges over populations and tolerances; the
results do not cover unbounded stakes or arbitrary control-variate processes.
\end{abstract}

\section{One-step information and total audit effort}
Shekhar et al.~\cite{shekhar2023} estimate a finite population's total monetary
misstatement by adaptive sampling without replacement. Their Proposition~2
identifies a sampling rule that minimizes the variance of the next
importance-weighted observation and maximizes a lower bound on one-step
wealth growth. They explicitly distinguish this surrogate from the harder
multistage problem of reducing the confidence-set width as quickly as possible.
We quantify that distinction: even with exact contribution information, the
repeated one-step rule can have the largest possible review-count ratio.

The mechanism concerns recorded value as well as misstatement. Reviewing a
large transaction can remove most of the unresolved monetary mass, even when
its actual misstatement is small. Contribution-proportional sampling can
postpone precisely that transaction. The proof retains the probabilistic
confidence set: two candidate totals survive every bounded bet until the
large transaction is reviewed. The stopping time then becomes its rank in a
probability-proportional sampling order.

\section{Confidence sets and admissible policies}
Let $\pi_i>0$, $\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\pi_i=1$, be normalized recorded values and
$f_i\in[0,1]$ fixed unknown misstatement fractions. The target is
$m^\star=\sum_i\pi_i f_i$. Before draw $t$, let $R_t$ be the remaining
indices and $A_{t-1}=\sum_{s<t}\pi_{I_s}f_{I_s}$ the observed contribution.
A predictable policy chooses $q_t(i)>0$ for every $i\in R_t$, with total
probability one, and observes $f_{I_t}$ after drawing $I_t\sim q_t$.
For each candidate $m\in[0,1]$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wealth}
\begin{split}
 Z_t&=\frac{\pi_{I_t}f_{I_t}}{q_t(I_t)},\qquad W_0(m)=1,\\
 W_t(m)&=W_{t-1}(m)\{1+\lambda_t(m)[Z_t-(m-A_{t-1})]\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Fix a betting-rule functional $B$ choosing predictable stakes for each policy
and history. The rule must make every wealth factor nonnegative under the
stipulated observation model and satisfy $|\lambda_t(m)|\le L<\infty$
uniformly over admissible instances, policies, histories, times, and candidates.
Full support gives $\E[Z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=m^\star-A_{t-1}$, so
$W_t(m^\star)$ is a nonnegative martingale. The usual maximal inequality
supplies simultaneous coverage at risk limit $\delta\in(0,1)$.

Put $A_t=\sum_{s\le t}\pi_{I_s}f_{I_s}$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:confidence}
\begin{split}
 J_t&=\left[A_t,A_t+\sum_{i\in R_{t+1}}\pi_i\right],\qquad J_0=[0,1],\\
 C_t&=\bigcap_{s=0}^{t}\left(J_s\cap\{m\in[0,1]:W_s(m)<1/\delta\}\right).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Thus the candidates are continuous, and both logical and running
intersections are part of the construction. For $0<\varepsilon<1$, set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:value}
 \tau_B(q)=\min\{1\le t\le N:\diam C_t\le\varepsilon\},\qquad
 V_B^\star=\inf_{q\in\mathcal Q_+}\E[\tau_B(q)],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal Q_+$ consists of the fully supported predictable policies
above. We use $\diam\varnothing=0$; an empty set can occur on a coverage-failure
event. Logical exhaustion ensures $\tau_B(q)\le N$. The same rule $B$ is
used for all policies in a comparison.

The oracle rule is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oracle}
 q_t^{\rm or}(i)=\frac{\pi_i f_i}{\sum_{j\in R_t}\pi_j f_j}.
\end{equation}
All worst-case comparisons below use populations with $f_i>0$, so this rule
has full support. It is a mathematical benchmark using unrevealed
contributions. The competing policies in our construction need only the
recorded weights. Infima in \eqref{eq:value} avoid assigning zero sampling
probability to a positive contribution. A deterministic review justified
solely by the logical interval is a separate valid procedure, but is
unnecessary for these comparisons.

\paragraph{A two-transaction example.}
Suppose the first stake is zero, as in the released ApproxKelly
implementation~\cite{implementation}. Take
\[
 (\pi_0,\pi_1)=(3/4,1/4),\qquad (f_0,f_1)=(1/3,1),\qquad
 \varepsilon=1/3.
\]
The betting set after one draw is $[0,1]$. Reviewing item~0 leaves logical
width $1/4$ and stops; reviewing item~1 leaves width $3/4$ and requires the
second review. Thus $\E_q\tau=2-q_1(0)$. Equal contributions make the oracle
expectation $3/2$, while recorded-value-proportional sampling gives $5/4$.
The fully supported choice $q_1=(1-\eta,\eta)$ gives $1+\eta$, so
$V_B^\star=1$. The general theorem does not require a zero first stake.

\section{A sharp worst-case theorem}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:sharp}
Fix $N\ge2$, $\delta\in(0,1)$, a finite $L\ge0$, and any betting rule $B$
satisfying the preceding assumptions. Choose a positive rational
$\varepsilon$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small}
 \varepsilon<1/3,\qquad (1+2L\varepsilon)^N<1/\delta.
\end{equation}
For any rational $\rho\in(0,1]$, set $a=\varepsilon/2$, $w=a/(N-1)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:family}
 \pi_0=1-a,\quad f_0=\frac{\rho w}{1-a};\qquad
 \pi_i=w,\quad f_i=1\quad(1\le i<N).
\end{equation}
These rational populations satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sharp}
 V_B^\star=1,\qquad \E[\tau_B(q^{\rm or})]=1+\frac{N-1}{1+\rho}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $N,\delta,L,B$, the supremum of
$\E[\tau_B(q^{\rm or})]/V_B^\star$ over positive-contribution populations
and tolerances $0<\varepsilon<1$ is exactly $N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A rational $\varepsilon$ satisfying \eqref{eq:small} exists because the
left-hand side tends to one as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. The population
\eqref{eq:family} is admissible: $a<1/6$, $w\le a$, and
$0<\rho w/(1-a)<1$. Its total contribution is $m^\star=a+\rho w$.

\emph{Two candidates prevent early stopping.}
Consider $m_-=a$ and $m_+=5a$, separated by $2\varepsilon$.
Before item~0 is reviewed, a history consists of $k$ small items,
$0\le k<N$, and has logical interval $[kw,1]$. Both candidates belong to
it. Under \eqref{eq:oracle},
\[
 Z_t=\sum_{i\in R_t}\pi_i f_i=m^\star-A_{t-1},\qquad
 Z_t-(m-A_{t-1})=m^\star-m.
\]
At either candidate, $|m^\star-m_\pm|<2\varepsilon$. Each nonnegative
wealth factor is at most $1+2L\varepsilon$, independently of the rule's
initialization or subsequent choices. At every preceding time,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:witness}
 W_t(m_\pm)\le(1+2L\varepsilon)^t
 \le(1+2L\varepsilon)^N<1/\delta.
\end{equation}
Both candidates survive every logical and betting intersection, preventing
stopping. Once item~0 is reviewed, the unreviewed recorded mass is at most
$a\le\varepsilon$, which forces stopping. The stopping time is therefore
exactly the rank of item~0.

\emph{Compute the rank.}
Successive sampling in proportion to fixed positive rates is equivalent to
ordering independent exponential clocks with those rates: the first clock
has the proportional distribution, and memorylessness repeats this after each
selection. Here the rates are $\rho w,w,\ldots,w$. Each small item precedes
item~0 with probability $w/(w+\rho w)=1/(1+\rho)$. Writing the rank as one
plus a sum of preceding-item indicators proves \eqref{eq:sharp}'s expectation.

\emph{Compare with fully supported policies.}
Give item~0 first-draw probability $1-\eta$ and each other item probability
$\eta/(N-1)$, then use any fully supported continuation. The first branch
stops in one review by the logical interval; all other branches use at most
$N$ reviews. Hence
\[
 1\le V_B^\star\le(1-\eta)+\eta N=1+\eta(N-1).
\]
Letting $\eta\downarrow0$ gives $V_B^\star=1$. Finally,
$1\le\tau_B(q)\le N$ gives the universal ratio bound $N$, and
\eqref{eq:sharp} approaches it as rational $\rho\downarrow0$.
\end{proof}

The theorem asserts an infimum of one, without a general nonattainment claim.
A fully supported policy with suitable valid bounded stakes can stop in one
review on every branch; the supplement gives an exact example that even
retains the true total on both branches. Nonattainment in the preceding
zero-first-stake example does not extend to the full betting class.

\begin{corollary}[Heterogeneous review costs]\label{cor:cost}
For known costs $c_i>0$, put
$C_B(q)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_B(q)}c_{I_t}$ and
$V_{B,c}^\star=\inf_{q\in\mathcal Q_+}\E[C_B(q)]$.
If $\max_i c_i/\min_i c_i\le\kappa$, $\kappa\ge1$, the sharp worst-case
supremum of $\E[C_B(q^{\rm or})]/V_{B,c}^\star$ is $1+(N-1)\kappa$.
Without bounded cost heterogeneity the ratio is unbounded, already for $N=2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Assign $c_0=1$ and $c_i=\kappa$ for $i\ge1$ in \eqref{eq:family}.
The rank calculation and fully supported approximation give
\[
 \E[C_B(q^{\rm or})]=1+\frac{(N-1)\kappa}{1+\rho},\qquad
 1\le V_{B,c}^\star\le1+\eta(N-1)\kappa.
\]
Thus $V_{B,c}^\star=1$, and $\rho\downarrow0$ yields the lower bound.
For any population, every audit costs at least $c_{\min}=\min_i c_i$ and
at most $\sum_i c_i\le c_{\min}[1+(N-1)\kappa]$, proving the upper bound.
For $N=2$ and fixed $\rho>0$, the displayed expectation diverges as
$\kappa\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

\section{Interpretation and limits}
The bound concerns the one-step rule of Shekhar et al., without contradicting
their one-step theorem. It holds even when sampling scores equal all realized
taints, since score-proportional sampling then equals \eqref{eq:oracle}.
A small contribution need not mean small unresolved recorded value.
Recorded-value-proportional sampling stops no later than item~0's rank on
\eqref{eq:family}, giving
\[
 \E[\tau_B(q^{\rm propM})]\le1+\frac{a}{1-a+w}.
\]
This is a fully supported, implementable comparator close to one when
$\varepsilon$ is small, without a claim of general optimality.

Confidence sequences for sampling without replacement were developed by
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas~\cite{waudby2020}; the adaptive monetary-weighted
construction here is from Shekhar et al.~\cite{shekhar2023}.
Imberg et al.~\cite{imberg2024} optimize asymptotic estimation error in active
finite-population sampling, a different objective from this stopping-time
ratio. Kato and Nakagawa~\cite{kato2026} compute sequential boundaries and
expected sample sizes for binary finite populations under simple random
sampling; they explicitly exclude monetary-unit and variables sampling.

The finite cap is essential to \eqref{eq:witness}; the argument does not
cover unbounded stakes or arbitrary control-variate wealth processes.
It allows the tolerance to decrease with $N$, $L$, and $\delta$. The sharp
supremum is over tolerances, not a factor-$N$ assertion for every prescribed
tolerance. Logical and running intersections are explicit assumptions.

If simultaneous intervals $\ell_i\le f_i\le u_i$ are supplied separately,
the residual box width after reviewing a set $A$ is
$\sum_{i\notin A}\pi_i(u_i-\ell_i)$. Sorting these widths minimizes the
review count for that certificate alone. This observation and its cost
extension are developed in the supplement, alongside the finite-grid solver
and synthetic experiments. None is needed for the sharp theorem.
